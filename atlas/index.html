About this atlas

The atlas maps where methane (CH₄) and related greenhouse gases have been observed in Africa: river and lake sampling campaigns, eddy-covariance and atmospheric towers, chambers, aircraft transects, total-column FTIR stations and flask sites. It starts from the Africa Methane database spreadsheet ( records) and grows as researchers add their own.

How to use it

  • Combine filters on the left. Options within one group widen the result (CH₄ or N₂O); different groups narrow it. The number beside each option shows how many records you would get.
  • Drag the year handles to keep records whose observation period overlaps that range.
  • Click a map point to see every record at that location. Click a table row to fly to it.
  • Tick “Only records inside the current map view” to use the map as a spatial filter.
  • “Download CSV” exports exactly the records you have filtered, with clean column names.

Data notes

How the infrastructure list was built (October 2026). The original spreadsheet was merged with the project shapefiles (curated operational, future and historical subsets from March 2026; the Yangambi and mobile flux towers; MOYA/ZWAMPS flight tracks), the African sites in NOAA ObsPack GLOBALVIEWplus v7 and its methane-isotope flask series, and the SPARK total-column and eddy-covariance site list. Records were matched within a few kilometres when they had the same observing system and a similar name or network. Gaps were filled without overwriting existing values, and an operational record was never downgraded by an older source. Every record has three standard fields: Status (Operational, Future or Ended; Future is split into funded/planned and candidate), Observing system (one of nine standard types) and Infrastructure type (fixed station, mobile platform or field campaign). The Data source filter shows where each record came from. Contact details from the project site list were not imported.

Integration log ( changes)

    How the map colours are organised. Each layer has its own colour family, so you can stack them without mixing them up. Sites are bold dots with a white ring. Footprints are contour lines only, in four dark hues: indigo (January), green (March), purple (July) and slate (October). Total CH₄ emissions use the only warm scale, yellow to dark red. Water is blue. HAND is teal and fades out on high ground. Elevation is a neutral stone grey with hill shading. Biomes and ecoregions use muted pastel versions of the RESOLVE colours, so the layers above them stay readable.

    Terrain and water layers (Map layers in the left panel), all clipped to Africa: Elevation comes from the open AWS Terrain Tiles (a blend of SRTM, GMTED2010 and ETOPO1), averaged to a 0.05° grid (about 5.5 km) and shaded for relief. Height above nearest drainage (HAND) was computed from that grid: D8 flow directions, a drainage network wherever more than about 600 km² drains to a cell, then each cell's height above the channel it drains to. At this resolution it shows regional relief, so read it as “low-lying, flood-prone ground” rather than field-scale wetness. Large flat basins such as the Congo Cuvette, Sudd, Inner Niger Delta, Lake Chad and Okavango, as well as some Saharan depressions, come out near zero. Water bodies combines Natural Earth 1:10m lakes, reservoirs and major rivers with smaller streams traced from the same elevation grid. In the Sahara these follow dry valleys (wadis).

    The Total CH₄ emissions layer (switch it in the left panel, under Map layers) is the total posterior methane flux (g CH₄ m⁻² month⁻¹) shown behind the project's footprint figure, rebuilt on its 1° grid by reading each cell's colour against the figure's colour scale. Values are accurate to about ±0.05 at low emissions and are capped at 2, the top of that scale. Grey cells are those the figure leaves uncoloured (very low or masked). Point at the map to read a cell's value.

    The Footprints layer shows the mean 3-day atmospheric footprints of five new total-column sites (Ikenne, Dja-Somalomo, Kigali, Assosa and Maun) for January, March, July and October, at contour levels of 10–50 ppm per kgC m⁻² h⁻¹. They were traced from the project's footprint figure (left-hand “Back 3 days” panels) and placed on the map using the sites' coordinates, so their outlines are accurate to roughly a quarter of a degree. The emission background and site labels from that figure are not included. The Kigali footprint was originally computed at 1.96° N, but the city lies at 1.96° S (1.9617° S, 30.0642° E). Its contours have been moved 3.92° south to the correct position (Oct 2026); where they join the Assosa (Ethiopia) footprint, the shift fades out smoothly towards Assosa so the shared contours stay continuous. This is an approximation: the shape still reflects the winds at the original point, so it should be replaced once the footprint model is re-run for the correct coordinates.

    The map background shows the RESOLVE Ecoregions 2017 dataset (Dinerstein et al. 2017, “An Ecoregions-Based Approach to Protecting Half the Terrestrial Realm”, BioScience 67:534–545, CC BY 4.0), clipped to Africa's land and simplified to about 1.5 km. Switch it on or off under Map layers in the left panel, where you can also show the individual ecoregions instead of biomes. Every record is tagged with the ecoregion and biome at its coordinates, so you can filter by biome and export both columns in the CSV. RESOLVE does not map large lakes, so records on Lake Victoria, Kivu, Tanganyika and similar are tagged “Open water (large lake)”. A few island or offshore records fall outside the mapped land.

    Each row of the spreadsheet is one record: a site, a system and an observation period. Several records often share a location, for example a tower and a chamber at the same station. Original IDs are shown where they exist. Every record also has a stable key (AM0002, …) based on its spreadsheet row.
